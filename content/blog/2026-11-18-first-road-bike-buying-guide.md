Your first road bike should make you want to ride more. That comes down to a handful of things — and most of them aren't on the spec sheet. Here's what matters, in order, and where your money goes furthest.

## 1. Start with fit, not specs

A bike that fits is comfortable for hours; one that doesn't will hurt your back, neck or knees no matter how light it is. Most brands size road bikes by rider height, so start with our [bike size guide](/guides/bike-size-guide/) or enter your height on any bike's page to see the size we suggest.

Then think about **geometry** — how stretched out you'll be:

- **Endurance bikes** (like the Trek Domane, Cannondale Synapse and Giant Defy) put you more upright. They're the easiest first road bike: comfortable on long rides, stable on descents and forgiving on rough roads.
- **Race bikes** (like the Trek Émonda, Cannondale SuperSix EVO and Giant TCR) are lower and more aggressive. Great if you already ride a lot and want speed; less comfortable if you're new.

If you're unsure, choose endurance. You can always lower the handlebars later.

## 2. Aluminium is a great first frame

Carbon frames are lighter and absorb more road buzz, but a good aluminium frame with a **carbon fork** is the sweet spot for a first bike. It's tough, affordable and leaves money for the parts that make a bigger difference: wheels, tires and a proper fit.

## 3. Pick a groupset with enough gears for your climbs

The groupset is your gears and brakes. Shimano's road range, from entry level up:

- **Claris** (8-speed) and **Sora** (9-speed): fine for commuting and flat rides.
- **Tiagra** (10-speed): a solid step up with smoother shifting.
- **105** (12-speed): the groupset many riders aim for. Light, precise and easy to maintain.

More important than the name is the **gear range**. If your rides include climbs — around Antipolo, Tanay or Marilaque — look for a compact crankset (50/34 teeth) with a wide cassette (11-32 or 11-34). Your knees will thank you.

## 4. Disc brakes are worth it here

Rim brakes work, but disc brakes stop far more consistently in the wet, and the rainy season here is long. **Hydraulic disc brakes** feel the best: strong, smooth and light at the lever. Mechanical discs are a good budget option.

## 5. Go wider on tires

Modern road bikes take wider tires, and on our roads that's a gift. **28–32 mm tires** are more comfortable over cracks and potholes, grip better in the rain and roll just as fast as skinny tires at real-world speeds. Check how wide the frame can go before you buy. If the wheels are tubeless-ready, that's a bonus — here's [why riders switch to tubeless](/blog/tubeless-tires-explained/).

## 6. Budget for the extras

Set aside something for what makes riding safe and comfortable:

- A **helmet** that fits well
- **Front and rear lights** — use them day and night
- A **lock**, **bottle cages** and a **floor pump**
- **Pedals**, if the bike doesn't come with them (check the spec list)
- Padded cycling shorts — the best comfort upgrade per peso

Depending on what you already have, plan for roughly ₱5,000–₱15,000 on top of the bike.

## Quick checklist

1. The right size for your height (and an endurance fit if you're new)
2. Aluminium frame with a carbon fork, or carbon if the budget allows
3. Enough low gears for your hills
4. Disc brakes, ideally hydraulic
5. Room for 28 mm tires or wider
6. Money set aside for a helmet, lights and pedals
